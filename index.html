Research

An anomaly is not always visible one observation at a time. A loaded die looks fair on any single throw and gives itself away only in the frequencies of many — and the same holds for fraud inside a plausible transaction history, for new fundamental interactions distorting the distributions of particle collisions, or for a language model whose behaviour has drifted in ways no single output reveals. Asking does what we observe match what our model of the world predicts? is one statistical problem, and classical tests answer it rigorously but lose power when deviations are rare or buried among uninformative features.

My research builds tests that scale to the complexity of modern data without giving up what makes a detection usable: a calibrated p-value, an account of which structure in the data produced it, and robustness to the uncertainties of the reference model itself.

Robust anomaly tests under systematic uncertainty Uncertainties built into the test rather than corrected for afterwards. Physics-inspired architectures and interpretability Sparsity, locality, and competition as the source of interpretability. Detection at scale, from raw data to p-values Representation learning paired with interpretable likelihood-ratio tests. Validating generative models Deciding when a generative surrogate can stand in for the real thing. Auditing and monitoring large AI models Detecting and characterizing harmful behaviour and domain drift.

Background

The question has been the same since my undergraduate thesis: how do you establish, rigorously, that data contain something your model did not predict? I trained as a physicist at the University of Padova and carried out my doctorate at CERN, the European laboratory for particle physics, as a CERN Doctoral Student in the CMS experiment — one of the two general-purpose detectors at the Large Hadron Collider. I was advised by Marco Zanetti and Andrea Wulzer and supervised at CERN by Maurizio Pierini. My thesis developed the New Physics Learning Machine, a model-agnostic test for departures from the Standard Model, and confronted what I still consider the central obstacle to using such methods for real measurements: keeping a p-value trustworthy when the reference model itself carries systematic uncertainty. I deployed the method on CMS collision data and on detector monitoring.

From 2023 to 2026 I held an IAIFI Fellowship — a competitively awarded independent postdoctoral fellowship, which gave me the freedom to define my own research programme across MIT and Harvard. I used it to take anomaly detection beyond the setting it came from: into learned representations, into the validation of generative models, and into the behaviour of large AI models. Over those years I was mentored by Philip Harris at MIT and Demba Ba at Harvard SEAS, and built collaborations across particle physics, statistics and machine learning.

I am now a Postdoctoral Associate at the MIT Laboratory for Nuclear Science. I remain a member of the CMS collaboration, having convened its ML Innovation group from 2023 to 2025, and I serve on the organizing team and Advisory Committee of PHYSTAT, the international forum where particle physicists and statisticians work out how inference should be done in the field. I co-organize VERAIPHY, which is setting out the statistical standards I would like work in this field to be held to.
